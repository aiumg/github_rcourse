Case Studies

Three patients, one assay, three different conclusions

In this chapter

  • Apply likelihood ratios to individual patients with different pre-test probabilities
  • Recognise when a test result should not change management
  • Check whether accuracy holds up in clinically relevant subgroups

Everything so far has described populations. Clinical work happens one patient at a time. These three attend the same department on the same evening and all have their troponin measured with the same assay, whose likelihood ratios we established in Chapter 3:

Positive LR = 3.4    Negative LR = 0.054
  • Case A — the low-risk patient
  • Case B — the intermediate patient
  • Case C — the high-risk patient

A 34-year-old woman, no cardiac risk factors, six hours of sharp left-sided chest pain that worsens on inspiration and is reproducible on palpation. ECG normal.

Her presentation is characteristic of musculoskeletal pain. A reasonable pre-test probability of infarction is around 1%.

pre_a  <- 0.01
post_a <- dtx_posterior(pre_a, m$lr_pos)   # if troponin comes back positive
round(c(pre_test = pre_a, post_test_if_positive = post_a), 3)
             pre_test post_test_if_positive 
                0.010                 0.033

Her troponin returns at 18 ng/L — above the threshold. Even so, her post-test probability is only 3.3%. Roughly 29 patients like her would test positive for every one genuinely infarcting.

The real error happened before the test

The mistake was not in interpreting the result but in ordering the test. In a patient with a 1% pre-test probability, a positive troponin is more likely to be a false alarm than a true finding — yet it now commits her to admission, serial testing and possibly angiography. Reproducible chest wall pain in a 34-year-old rarely warrants a troponin at all.

A 58-year-old man, hypertensive and a smoker, two hours of central pressing chest pain radiating to the jaw, now settled. ECG shows non-specific T-wave flattening.

This is the genuinely uncertain case. Pre-test probability perhaps 25%.

pre_b <- 0.25
c(if_positive = dtx_posterior(pre_b, m$lr_pos),
  if_negative = dtx_posterior(pre_b, m$lr_neg)) |> round(3)
if_positive if_negative 
      0.531       0.018

Here the test earns its place. A positive result takes him to 53.1% and mandates admission; a negative result drops him to 1.8%, low enough to consider discharge with follow-up.

Where tests are worth ordering

A test changes management only when the result can move the patient across a decision threshold. Case B starts between the treat and discharge thresholds, so either result is actionable. That is the definition of a useful test.

A 71-year-old diabetic man, 40 minutes of crushing chest pain, diaphoretic, with 2 mm ST elevation in the inferior leads.

Pre-test probability is around 95%. The ECG has already made the diagnosis.

pre_c <- 0.95
dtx_posterior(pre_c, m$lr_neg) |> round(3)
[1] 0.508

Even a negative troponin leaves him at 50.8%. Troponin also takes hours to rise, so an early sample may be negative in an evolving infarct.

Do not wait for the test

He goes to the catheter laboratory on the ECG and the history. Waiting for troponin would delay reperfusion without changing the decision — the textbook example of a test that cannot move a patient across the treatment threshold and therefore should not delay treatment.

1 Does accuracy hold in every subgroup?

Sensitivity and specificity are stable across prevalence, but not necessarily across case mix. Worth checking directly.

subgroup <- function(rows, label) {
  d <- cohort[rows, ]
  t <- dtx_table(d$test_positive, d$mi_confirmed)
  s <- dtx_measures(t)
  data.frame(Subgroup = label, n = t$n,
             Prevalence = pct(s$prevalence),
             Sensitivity = pct(s$sensitivity),
             Specificity = pct(s$specificity),
             PPV = pct(s$ppv))
}

results <- rbind(
  subgroup(rep(TRUE, nrow(cohort)),          "All patients"),
  subgroup(cohort$sex == "F",                "Women"),
  subgroup(cohort$sex == "M",                "Men"),
  subgroup(cohort$hours_since_onset < 3,     "Presenting < 3 h"),
  subgroup(cohort$hours_since_onset >= 3,    "Presenting >= 3 h"),
  subgroup(cohort$age < 60,                  "Age under 60"),
  subgroup(cohort$age >= 60,                 "Age 60 and over")
)
How to read this table

Look across the prevalence column, then across sensitivity and specificity. The prevalence differences do not drag sensitivity and specificity with them — that is the independence from Chapter 2, visible in real numbers. PPV, by contrast, tracks prevalence closely.

Where sensitivity does differ between subgroups, suspect a genuine biological difference in the test’s behaviour rather than a prevalence effect. Early presentation is the classic example: troponin needs time to rise, so tests taken within a few hours of symptom onset genuinely miss more infarctions. Small subgroups also produce unstable estimates, so check the denominators before concluding anything.

3 Check yourself

Case C has a pre-test probability of 95% and a negative troponin. What is the correct action?

His post-test probability remains around 80%, far above any threshold for treatment, and troponin may not yet have risen in an infarct of 40 minutes’ duration. A test that cannot move the patient across a decision threshold should not delay treatment.

In the subgroup table, PPV differs markedly between women and men while sensitivity is similar. The most likely explanation is:

PPV depends on prevalence; sensitivity does not. When sensitivity and specificity are stable but PPV moves, prevalence is the first explanation to consider. A genuine difference in assay performance would show up in sensitivity or specificity as well.
