Sensitivity and Specificity

What the test does, independent of who is being tested

1 Two questions, two denominators

In this chapter

  • Derive sensitivity and specificity and identify their denominators
  • Attach a confidence interval to each and explain why it is often wide
  • Use SnNout and SpPin correctly, and know when they mislead
  • Explain why these two measures alone cannot interpret a patient’s result

Sensitivity and specificity split the 2×2 table down its columns. Each asks about a group of patients defined by whether they have the disease.

Sensitivity asks: among patients who do have the disease, what proportion does the test detect?

\[ \text{Sensitivity} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \]

Specificity asks: among patients who do not have the disease, what proportion does the test correctly clear?

\[ \text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP} \]

Both denominators are column totals. Neither involves the number of patients the test called positive, and neither depends on how common the disease is. This is the property that makes them portable between settings — and, as Chapter 3 shows, the property that makes them nearly useless at the bedside on their own.

2 Computing them

sensitivity <- tab$tp / (tab$tp + tab$fn)
specificity <- tab$tn / (tab$tn + tab$fp)

c(sensitivity = round(sensitivity, 3),
  specificity = round(specificity, 3))
sensitivity specificity 
      0.961       0.717

In our cohort the troponin threshold of 14 ng/L detects 96.1% of confirmed infarctions and correctly clears 71.7% of those without one.

Sanity check

74 true positives divided by 77 patients with confirmed MI gives 0.961. If a calculated sensitivity ever exceeds 1, you have used a row total as the denominator.

3 A point estimate is not enough

Both measures are proportions estimated from a sample, so both carry uncertainty. The denominator for sensitivity is only the diseased patients — here 77 of 600 — so the interval around it is usually much wider than the one around specificity.

Reporting sensitivity as a bare percentage hides this. A test described as “92% sensitive” from a study of 25 diseased patients is compatible with a true sensitivity below 75%.

4 SnNout and SpPin

Two mnemonics from evidence-based medicine, useful when their conditions hold:

  • SnNout — with a highly Snsitive test, a Negative result rules the diagnosis out
  • SpPin — with a highly Specific test, a Positive result rules it in

The logic is sound. A test that almost never misses the disease produces few false negatives, so a negative result is informative.

Where these mnemonics fail

Both are shortcuts around Bayes’ theorem, and both quietly assume the pre-test probability is not extreme. A 99% sensitive test does not rule out a disease in a patient whose pre-test probability was 90% to begin with — a negative result leaves them well above the threshold at which you would act. Use the mnemonics to build intuition, not to make decisions. Chapter 3 gives the arithmetic that always works.

5 Why neither answers your patient’s question

Your patient does not ask “if I had an infarction, would this test have caught it?” They ask “I have a positive result — do I have an infarction?”

That reverses the conditioning. Sensitivity is \(P(\text{test}^+ \mid \text{disease})\); the question asks for \(P(\text{disease} \mid \text{test}^+)\). These are different quantities and confusing them is the prosecutor’s fallacy (Altman and Bland 1994).

In our cohort sensitivity is 96.1%, but the probability that a patient with a positive troponin actually infarcted is 33.3% — a very different number, and it depends on something sensitivity deliberately ignores.

6 Check yourself

A screening test has sensitivity 0.95 and specificity 0.80. It is moved from a hospital population to a community population where the disease is ten times rarer. What happens to the sensitivity?

Sensitivity is conditional on having the disease, so changing how many people have it does not change the proportion detected. The caveat is real though: if the community cases are milder than the hospital cases, sensitivity can genuinely fall — that is spectrum bias, not a prevalence effect.

Which single change would most reduce the number of false negatives?

Lowering the threshold calls more patients positive, so fewer diseased patients are missed: sensitivity rises. The price is more false positives and lower specificity. Chapter 4 traces that trade-off across every possible threshold.
