Why is a Wilson interval preferred to the normal approximation for sensitivity?

The normal approximation can produce intervals extending beyond 1, or absurdly narrow ones, exactly where diagnostic accuracy estimates usually sit. Narrower is not better if the coverage is wrong.

A test has sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.90. Its positive likelihood ratio is:

LR⁺ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity) = 0.80 / 0.10 = 8.0. The 0.22 is the negative likelihood ratio, (1 − 0.80) / 0.90.

3 Section C — prevalence and Bayes

Which quantities change when the same test is moved to a population where the disease is rarer?

Sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios and AUC all describe the test and are independent of prevalence. Predictive values describe what a result means in a given population, and move with it.

A patient’s pre-test probability is 40%. A test with a negative likelihood ratio of 0.1 comes back negative. The post-test probability is closest to:

Pre-test odds = 0.4/0.6 = 0.667. Post-test odds = 0.667 × 0.1 = 0.0667. Probability = 0.0667/1.0667 = 0.063, about 6%. The intuitive answer of 4% comes from multiplying the probability directly, which is the error the odds conversion exists to prevent.

Screening a low-prevalence population with a highly accurate test typically produces:

The false positives come from the large healthy majority. Even a 1% false positive rate applied to hundreds of thousands of healthy people swamps the small number of genuine cases. This is the central difficulty in every screening programme.

4 Section D — thresholds and ROC

Lowering a diagnostic threshold will:

More patients are called positive, so fewer cases are missed and more healthy patients are wrongly flagged. Every point on an ROC curve is one such trade.

The area under the ROC curve represents:

It is a threshold-free measure of discrimination, not of accuracy. A test can have an excellent AUC and still be useless at the cut-off you actually use, or in the population you actually serve.

Youden weights the two errors equally, which is not the clinical reality here. When a false negative is many times more costly than a false positive, the optimum moves to a lower threshold — as the cost curves in Chapter 4 show.

A colleague reports that a new biomarker has “94% accuracy”. What is the most useful next question?

Overall accuracy is dominated by prevalence. In a population where 6% have the disease, a test that calls everyone negative achieves 94% accuracy while detecting nothing. Accuracy alone is close to uninterpretable without the components behind it.
